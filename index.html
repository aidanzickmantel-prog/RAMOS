RAMOS · Residue, Allocation, Mapping, Optimisation, Sustainability

Every residue in Portugal. Mapped, priced and allocated where it removes the most carbon at the lowest cost.

RAMOS is a national spatial decision model for organic residues. It locates every tonne where it arises, records its current fate and the limits on its removal, and then optimises which conversion pathway receives it, where plants are sited, at what scale, and with what economic and carbon outcome.

Act 0 · Why

LeftoversOrganic residues in Portugal: burned, abandoned or landfilled

requests to burn residue in Portugal, 2020 to 2025

Organic waste from our farms, our forests and our own bins has three fates. It is burned, it rots, or it is buried in landfill.

Prunings go up in smoke in the field. Forest slash is left to rot and feed the next fire. Food waste ends up in landfill. Either way, the carbon those plants pulled from the air goes straight back into the atmosphere. All that hard work by plants, wasted.

Agriculture, forestry and municipalities generate millions of tonnes of organic residue each year. Most of it is not put to productive use.

Agricultural prunings are burned in the open. Forest harvest residues remain on site and add to wildfire fuel loads. Municipal biowaste is largely landfilled. In each case the biogenic carbon returns to the atmosphere and the material's value is lost.

Act 0 · Why

Burned, buried, wasted

That is the story everywhere. Here is the evidence, source by source.

In the towns, of food and garden waste a year never reaches a composter or a digester. Only of biowaste is collected separately. On the farms, around half of olive and vine prunings are burned in the open. In the forests, what is left after cutting dries out and becomes fuel for the next fire.

Act 0 · Why

Closing the land's loops

Climate change asks for two things at once: cut emissions and pull carbon back out of the air. Portugal's 2024 inventory still counts before land use.

Residues can help with both. Digestion turns wet waste into biomethane and returns digestate to the fields. Pyrolysis locks carbon into biochar for soils and concrete. Composting and gasification close other loops. This is industrial ecology: an economy that works like an ecosystem, where one process feeds the next.

Each loop is judged by life cycle assessment, from the field to the end use. Our biochar study alone found , or of the 2050 target it used.

Act 0 · Why

The same tonne, counted twiceCarbon removal potentials are double-counted

Climate plans count the same biomass more than once.

Models assess each removal method on its own: biochar, bioenergy with carbon capture, biomethane. Each assumes the residues are all its own, so the potentials add up to more biomass than exists. Our two earlier studies did it too: both used the same municipal food waste.

RAMOS gives each tonne to one pathway, chosen to remove the most carbon at the lowest cost. As far as we know, no one has yet optimised these residues across every removal pathway at once, place by place.

Carbon removal studies usually assess each pathway on its own and assume the whole residue resource is available to it. Summed across biochar, biomethane and bioenergy, these potentials exceed the biomass that exists.

RAMOS allocates each tonne to a single pathway. The allocation maximises carbon removal at the lowest cost, location by location.

Act 0 · Why

What should become of Portugal's residues?

RAMOS is a spatial decision model for planning sustainability projects that turn residues into carbon removal and renewable energy.

It maps where each residue arises, records what happens to it today and applies the limits set by soil and by law. Then biochar, biomethane, gasification, composting and leaving it in place compete for every tonne. Each tonne is allocated once, so no carbon is counted twice.

The aim is to manage these natural resources within what the land can give, and to plan a better future from evidence.

Ramos is Portuguese for branches.

How it works

How RAMOS works

Step 1 of 7

Resource mapping

Residues are located at their source: declared farm parcels, 10 m forest cover, mills, livestock farms and waste treatment plants.

Step 2 of 7

Current use

Each stream carries its present fate: open burning, mulching, firewood or sale. Only residue without an existing use enters the allocation.

Step 3 of 7

Sustainable removal limits

A share of the residue stays on the field to maintain soil organic carbon, soil cover and nutrient return. On slopes above 25% all of it stays, to protect against erosion. Natura 2000 and national protected areas are excluded, and machine reach sets the technical limit. Only what remains can be collected.

Step 4 of 7

Collection logistics

In-field chipping and vehicle routing on the national road network, costed per kilometre and per operating hour.

Step 5 of 7

Technology, siting and scale

Pyrolysis plants, mobile units, anaerobic digesters and gasifiers compete for the same feedstock. Plant scale is optimised jointly with the catchment, balancing economies of scale against rising transport cost.

Step 6 of 7

End use

Biochar is routed to agricultural soils and concrete, biomethane to the gas grid, and power and heat to nearby demand.

Step 7 of 7

Outcomes

Cost, revenue, carbon removal and avoided emissions are reported as separate indicators, never aggregated into a single figure.

A single mass balance links every stage, so no tonne is allocated twice.

What makes it different

What sets RAMOS apart.

01

Single allocation of a finite resource

To our knowledge, the first national model in which all carbon removal pathways compete for the same finite residue, location by location, without double counting.

02

Chipping beats burning. Biochar beats both.

Burn a tonne of prunings and you release about 198 kg CO2e of methane and nitrous oxide on top of the carbon. Chip it and leave it, and most of it rots away. The soil keeps about 190 kg CO2e. Turn it into biochar and you lock away 422 to 735 kg CO2e, depending on which rules you count by, for the cost of 30 to 60 kg in collection and processing.

03

Uncertainty reported as a result

Biochar value, carbon price, gas price and feedstock price each carry a base value and a range. Results are reported across those ranges, which is the basis for investment decisions.

Full traceability

Every parameter is graded and traced to its source, every run is reproducible, and nearly 3,000 automated tests verify results whenever the model changes.

Same tonne, opposite signOne tonne of olive prunings, three fates

Take a tonne that would have been burned, and you stop the methane and nitrous oxide from the fire: saved.

Take a tonne that would have been chipped and left, and you take carbon the soil would have kept: net over 100 years. Same lorry, same material, opposite sign. RAMOS never averages the two.

Act 2 · The parcel

How much may be taken

Soil needs some of the residue. Steep ground needs cover. Machines cannot reach everything, and some land is protected by law.

RAMOS treats each limit separately and gives the removable share as a range. Different land managers care about different things, so the user chooses whose concerns count.

Act 3 · Collection

Chipped at the roadside, then hauled

Farmers stack their prunings at the edge of the farm track. A crew arrives with a chip truck and a towed chipper, the set-up arborists use.

The chipper is hitched behind the truck. Two people feed the branches in by hand, and a straight chute blows the chips through the open rear door of the chip box. Chips pack far tighter than loose branches: . When the box is full, the truck takes the farm track to the road and on to the plant.

Act 3 · Collection

Farm to farm to plant

One truck, one morning. It stops at a pile, chips it, moves to the next farm along the dirt track, then the next.

When the body is full it heads to the plant on real roads. Watch every farm that feeds this plant light up in turn. RAMOS routes each run on the actual road network, prices it and counts its emissions.

Act 4 · The plant on the map

A plant sized to its catchment

Zoom out and the threads form a catchment.

RAMOS groups parcels into clusters and sizes each plant to what its own cluster can supply. Bigger is not automatically better: past a point, every extra tonne travels further than the savings are worth. Depots appear where a longer haul pays.

Act 5 · Inside the plants

The biochar plant

One wet tonne of olive prunings comes in. First the water is taken out of the arithmetic, because yields are measured on dry wood.

Heated without oxygen, a share of of the dry wood becomes biochar, and most of that is carbon. The plant reports how much carbon is physically in the char. How much of it counts is decided later, by a named method.

Act 5 · Inside the plants

The biomethane plant

Food waste and manure are too wet to burn or char. In a digester, microbes turn them into biogas.

Cleaned to grid quality, it becomes biomethane and flows into the gas network. What is left, the digestate, goes back to the fields. The engine also lets digestate feed a pyrolysis plant, so one pathway's output becomes another's input.

Act 5 · Inside the plants

The gasifier

Dry woody residue can also be gasified: heated with little oxygen into a fuel gas.

That gas runs an engine for power and heat, or is upgraded to synthetic methane or liquid fuels. Each configuration takes different feedstocks. Olive wood, for example, carries too much ash for a small gasifier.

Act 6 · Competition and end use

The auctionCarbon price and biochar viability

Think of each tonne as up for sale. Every pathway makes an offer: the most it could pay for that tonne and still break even. The best offer above zero takes it. If nobody can pay, the tonne stays on the farm.

A carbon price changes the offers. Biochar locks carbon away, so its offer climbs as the price rises. In one olive zone it starts winning at some carbon price. That crossing price is the answer RAMOS gives, per feedstock and per place.

Across today's carbon removal prices (108 to 130 EUR/t), the payable share of the olive biochar pool barely moves. Just above the top of that range it climbs steeply.

RAMOS shows exactly where that cliff sits, per feedstock and per place.

Act 6 · End use and impacts

Where the products goEnd uses: soils and concrete

Read the map in four steps. Residue goes to a plant. Biochar goes to farm soils and cement kilnsconcrete. Biomethane joins the gas grid. Then the emitters that need removals appear.

On the other side are the emitters: industrial sites, airports and ports that need removals. RAMOS can show how much of Portugal's remaining emissions these residues could cover. It never promises neutrality.

Biochar returns to agricultural soils, where it improves water and nutrient retention, particularly when co-applied with digestate or manure. It is also used in concrete.

Portugal produces about 7.5 million cubic metres of ready-mix concrete a year. Replacing less than 4% of the sand with biochar would absorb the entire national supply provisional. Biomethane is injected into the gas grid.

Act 6 · End use and impacts

Back into the soil

Biochar is spread on the field. Rain, roots and worms carry it down into the soil, where it breaks down very slowly. How much still counts depends on whom you ask.

A physical estimate, the EU credit rules and the submitted paper give different answers for the same char. RAMOS keeps them side by side and names the method on every result. Carbon removed and emissions avoided are never added into one number.

Act 6 · End use and impacts

Biodiversity response

Taking residue changes life on the land. On one plot, the left half is cleared of every branch. The right half keeps a share on the floor, and life comes back: fungi on the chips, beetles in deadwood, birds in the trees.

RAMOS adds biodiversity as its own lens beside carbon and cost. Some places are off limits. The indicator is still being built with colleagues, and the site says so.

Act 7 · What it is for

What RAMOS can be used for

RAMOS is built to be asked questions.

A municipality can ask where a plant should go and what it would gather. A researcher can ask what each pathway needs to win. A planner can ask how much of the national gap residues could cover, at what cost and with what effect on biodiversity.

Every number carries its source, every run can be reproduced exactly, and an independent audit checks the work.
